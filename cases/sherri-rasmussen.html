Sherri Rasmussen: The Bite-Mark DNA That Broke a 23-Year-Old Case

The 1986 killing was initially pursued as a burglary. Nearly two decades later, preserved biological evidence from a bite mark produced a female DNA profile. In 2009, investigators matched it to LAPD detective Stephanie Lazarus.

The 1986 scene

Rasmussen was killed in her condominium on February 24, 1986. The scene included signs of a violent struggle. Investigators initially pursued a burglary theory. Her parents had urged police to consider her husband John Ruetten’s former girlfriend, an LAPD officer, but the case went cold.

Evidence that survived

The coroner had preserved a bite-mark tissue/swab sample. When the cold-case unit revisited it, 2005 testing yielded a complete major DNA profile from a female. A national database search did not identify a match, so investigators later focused on women who might have had a reason to harm Rasmussen.

Why Lazarus became a focus

Court records describe Lazarus’s prior relationship with Ruetten, her distress over his engagement to Rasmussen and a confrontation with Rasmussen. Investigators also considered firearm evidence: the murder weapon was consistent with a gun Lazarus had owned, and ammunition was of a type required by LAPD at the time. She reported a similar gun stolen shortly after the homicide.

The DNA match

Detectives covertly obtained Lazarus’s discarded DNA and compared it with the bite-mark profile. The appellate opinion says the profile precisely matched the person who bit Rasmussen near the time of her death; trial testimony placed the random-match probability for an unrelated woman at approximately one in 1.7 sextillion.

Trial and appeal

Lazarus was convicted of first-degree murder and sentenced to 27 years to life. The California Court of Appeal affirmed the judgment in 2015. The appellate record also discusses partial DNA profiles from Rasmussen’s fingernails, defense challenges, unidentified DNA elsewhere at the scene and the court’s reasoning on third-party-culpability evidence.

What makes this case important

It is a textbook example of why evidence preservation matters. A biological sample that could not solve the case in 1986 became central once DNA science advanced—and the appellate record shows why forensic evidence must be interpreted alongside motive, opportunity and competing explanations rather than treated as a magic answer.
